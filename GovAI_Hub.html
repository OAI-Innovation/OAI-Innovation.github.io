RequirementNSW AI Operational Policy

Mandatory for all agencies using AI, under Circular DCS-2026-02.

Your agency must attest annually

Agencies provide a signed annual attestation to NSW GovAI by 31 October each year for the previous financial year. The first attestation is due by 31 October 2027, which gives agencies at least 12 months to implement the policy.

Compliance is mandatory for all agencies using AI. The policy is mandated by Circular DCS-2026-02, Use of Artificial Intelligence by NSW Government Agencies. It sets the acceptable use principles, the accountability expected of agencies, and the requirement to assess and refer AI use cases.

Read the policyRead Circular DCS-2026-02
RequirementUnderstand your responsibilitiesWhat employees, Accountable Officials and governance functions are each accountable for.
What every employee is responsible for

The policy applies to AI you use in your work, including tools embedded in software you already have. It is not limited to projects.

Do this

  • Check your agency ICT Acceptable Use policy, which must align to the acceptable use principles in the AI Operational Policy.
  • Assume a tool with an AI feature is in scope until someone confirms otherwise.
  • Never put personal, health, confidential or security-classified information into a public AI tool.
  • Check AI output before you rely on it. You remain accountable for the work, not the tool.
  • Tell the customer or colleague when AI materially shaped what they are receiving.
  • Complete the AI literacy and policy training your agency makes available.

What good looks like

You can explain, in your own words, what the tool did, what you checked, and what you decided.

Accountable Official responsibilities

Each agency has an Accountable Official for AI. The role is about oversight of the whole portfolio, not sign-off on individual tools.

  • Ensure AI use across the agency is identified, assessed under the AIAF and recorded in the agency AI register.
  • Ensure high-risk and critical-risk use cases are referred to the AI Review Committee for advice.
  • Ensure agency ICT Acceptable Use policies align to the acceptable use principles in the policy.
  • Ensure AI literacy and policy training is available to employees using AI in their work.
  • Provide the signed annual attestation to NSW GovAI by 31 October.

Common gap. AI already embedded in purchased software is often missed. The policy and the AIAF apply to AI components whether or not they arrived through a project.

Governance and assurance responsibilities

Governance functions make sure assessment actually happens, and that what was assessed is what went live.

  • Maintain the agency AI register and keep it current as systems change.
  • Check AIAF assessments are completed, signed off and stored in the agency records management system.
  • Confirm mandatory assurance activities flagged by the assessment are done, such as a Privacy Impact Assessment, Human Rights Impact Assessment or cyber security review.
  • Trigger a re-assessment when features, datasets, purposes or decision contexts change.
  • Track whether projects over $5 million or funded through the Digital Restart Fund have met the additional oversight required under the NSW Digital Assurance Framework.

Common policy questions

Does this apply to a tool we bought rather than built?

Yes. All NSW Government use of AI must apply the AIAF, including any AI system or component across its full lifecycle, whether or not it is part of a formal project.

We already assessed it last year. Do we do it again?

Re-run the AIAF whenever features, datasets, purposes or decision contexts change.

Do we wait for the AIAF Platform?

No. During the transition, agencies continue to apply the existing Excel-based assessment to all AI use cases until the platform is fully adopted.

Who decides in the end?

The agency. AI Review Committee advice is advisory; agencies retain full decision-making authority and accountability.

Complete an AIAF assessment

The mandatory risk self-assessment every AI use case must go through. Currently completed in the AIAF Excel workbook while the platform is being adopted.

Start an assessment
What the AIAF asks you to do

The AIAF is a risk-based self-assessment mapped to the NSW AI Ethics Principles. It produces a risk rating and a list of things you must then do.

How the workbook runs

  • Your answers generate an overall risk level and list the mandatory assurance activities that follow, such as a Privacy Impact Assessment, Human Rights Impact Assessment or cyber security review.
  • A deep dive section is optional but recommended. Risks you identify there are added to the risk register automatically.
  • Post-assessment actions are mandatory: record sign-off in the audit record, save the file in your agency records management system, and register high or critical use cases in the agency AI register.

Two things teams get wrong

  • Treating it as a one-off. Re-run it whenever features, datasets, purposes or decision contexts change.
  • Assessing the design rather than the system as built. The assessment should match what actually shipped.
How assessment and assurance fit together

Assessment tells you the risk. Assurance is what you then have to evidence, and to whom.

  • The AIAF sits inside the NSW Digital Assurance Framework.
  • Projects over $5 million, or funded through the Digital Restart Fund, receive additional central oversight to confirm AIAF compliance and alignment with NSW AI governance requirements.
  • Assessment outputs are records. They belong in your records management system, not a team drive.
  • Your agency remains accountable for identifying, assessing, managing and monitoring the risk.
What the AI Review Committee does

The AIRC brings together independent experts and public servants to review high-risk AI systems and give practical, risk-informed guidance.

  • Its advice supports safe, lawful and responsible implementation.
  • The advice is advisory. Agencies retain full decision-making authority and accountability.
  • It is chaired by Professor Edward Santow, Co-Director of the Human Technology Institute at UTS.
  • Referrals are coordinated by NSW GovAI through the AI Secretariat.

More: AI Review Committee on Digital NSW

When to refer to the AI Review Committee

Referral is not a judgement call. It follows from the assessment result.

Refer when

  • Your completed AIAF assessment produces a high or critical risk rating, after mitigations and controls are applied.

Before you send it

  • The assessment is complete, including the post-assessment actions.
  • Sign-off is recorded in the audit record.
  • The use case is registered in your agency AI register.
  • Your mitigations and controls are documented, not just intended.

Then

Send the completed self-assessment to the AI Secretariat, which coordinates the review. Email AISecretariat@customerservice.nsw.gov.au.

If the rating changes later, refer then. A system that gained new data, new autonomy or a new decision context is not the system that was assessed.

GuidanceExperimental AI capabilities requiring expert advice

These capabilities must not be used in production without expert advice.

Computer-use AI

AI that can see and control a computer interface, including clicking, typing, opening applications or changing information. Mistakes or malicious instructions can result in real actions.

Agentic browsers

AI that independently navigates websites, reads external content and may take actions. Web content is untrusted and can contain prompt injections designed to manipulate the agent.

Autonomous agents

AI that plans and completes multi-step tasks with limited human intervention, potentially over extended periods. Greater autonomy increases the potential consequence of incorrect or unexpected behaviour.

Agents connected to tools and systems

AI with APIs, connectors, plugins, MCP tools or application permissions that can read or change government systems. Risk increases significantly with write access, sensitive data or broad permissions.

Code-executing agents

AI that can create and execute code, scripts or terminal commands, or modify applications and infrastructure. Execution privileges can turn an AI error or compromised instruction into a technical or cyber incident.

Multi-agent and delegated systems

AI systems that assign tasks to other agents or create sub-agents. Delegation can make authority, provenance and accountability harder to trace and can propagate errors between agents.

Agents with persistent memory

AI that stores information and uses it in later tasks. Incorrect or malicious information can persist and influence future actions, while retained information can create privacy and security risks.

  2. Risk controls

Governance and accountability

Why it matters

AI will not scale safely if ownership, accountability and oversight are unclear. Agencies need visibility of where AI is being used and who is responsible for decisions about it.

What to do

  1. Assign clear accountability for AI governance and implementation.
  2. Make AI part of existing governance and assurance arrangements rather than creating disconnected processes.
  3. Maintain visibility of AI use cases being explored, developed and used across the agency.
  4. Ensure higher-risk use cases follow the required assessment and escalation pathways.
  5. Review governance processes that create unnecessary delay and simplify them where requirements can still be met.

What good looks like

AI activity is visible to the organisation, accountable owners are clear, and decisions can be escalated when risk or uncertainty increases.

Apply proportionate risk controls

Why it matters

AI controls should reflect the risk of the use case and continue across design, procurement, implementation and operation. Applying the same controls to every use case creates unnecessary friction, while inadequate controls can leave material risks unmanaged.

What to do

  1. Assess AI risk early and revisit it when the solution, data, purpose or operating context changes.
  2. Define what AI use is acceptable within your organisation and make this clear to staff.
  3. Match controls to the level and nature of risk rather than applying a single control set to every use case.
  4. Require appropriate human oversight where AI contributes to higher-impact decisions or actions.
  5. Consider risks introduced by vendors, models, external services and other parts of the AI supply chain.
  6. Include AI risk and control requirements when buying or contracting for AI-enabled services.

What good looks like

Teams understand which controls apply, why they apply and when additional oversight is required. Controls are proportionate, visible and applied across the solution lifecycle.

Prepare your data for AI

Why it matters

AI can amplify weaknesses in data as well as its value. Before relying on AI, teams need to understand whether the required data can be accessed lawfully, is fit for purpose and is sufficiently trusted.

What to do

  1. Identify the datasets the AI use case depends on.
  2. Confirm who is accountable for those datasets.
  3. Check whether the data can be lawfully accessed, used and shared for the proposed purpose.
  4. Understand data quality, known gaps and limitations before using it in an AI solution.
  5. Record where important data comes from and how it is transformed or used.
  6. Apply appropriate information classification, privacy and handling requirements.
  7. Use a controlled environment when sensitive or uncertain data is being used for experimentation.

What good looks like

Teams know what data they are relying on, who is accountable for it, whether it can lawfully be used and what limitations need to be managed.

Build workforce capability and confidence

Why it matters

AI adoption depends on people understanding where AI can help, how to use it appropriately and when specialist advice is needed. Capability is required across the whole workforce, not only technical teams.

What to do

  1. Establish a baseline level of AI literacy for employees.
  2. Give staff clear guidance on acceptable and safe use of AI at work.
  3. Build deeper AI governance and assurance capability in the roles responsible for oversight.
  4. Ensure executives understand AI opportunities, risks and their accountability.
  5. Give staff access to approved ways to experiment and learn safely.
  6. Provide a clear route for staff to ask questions, raise concerns or seek specialist advice.
  7. Share practical examples and lessons so capability grows from real implementation experience.

What good looks like

Staff know what they can use, what they are accountable for and where to get help. Leaders can make informed AI decisions and specialist functions can support delivery rather than only review it.

Establish secure and scalable AI platforms

Why it matters

Agencies need approved technology environments that allow people to test and use AI while maintaining appropriate access, visibility and control.

What to do

  1. Provide approved AI platforms or environments that staff can access for legitimate business needs.
  2. Define which data and information classifications each environment can handle.
  3. Integrate AI access with organisational identity and access controls.
  4. Log and monitor AI activity at a level appropriate to the use case and risk.
  5. Establish visibility of usage and operating cost.
  6. Define requirements for model deployment, hosting and data residency where relevant.
  7. Design a clear pathway from experimentation to production rather than treating a sandbox as the production solution.
  8. Review platform settings and controls as AI capability changes.

What good looks like

Teams can experiment and deliver using approved environments, while the agency retains visibility of access, usage, cost and material risk.

Requirements become evaluations

AI requirements should describe the result that must be demonstrated, not just the function the system performs. The artefact your project runs on changes shape.

How we used to write it

“The system will extract invoice data and route invoices to the correct workflow.”

→

What we have to agree instead

“On representative invoices, the system must process 90% without correction and route all uncertain or invalid cases for human review.”

What that changes for you

A new deliverable

The evaluation set becomes a core project artefact.

Shared ownership

The business defines what good looks like; technical teams prove the system meets it.

A new gate

Agreed performance thresholds determine whether the AI can go live.

What an evaluation set is

An evaluation set is a fixed collection of representative cases with the answer you expect, used to measure whether the AI performs well enough. It is the AI equivalent of a test pack, and it is the evidence behind any claim that the system works.

What it needs to contain
ElementWhy it matters
Representative casesDrawn from real work, including the awkward ones, not a tidy sample.
Expected resultsWhat a competent person would produce, agreed by the business.
Held-out casesCases the build team did not tune against, so the score means something.
Known hard casesThe failures you already worry about, so you find out early.
A named ownerSomeone keeps it current as the work and the data change.

Include in your definition of done

Five AI-specific checks to add to your production definition of done.

  1. Meets the agreed thresholdon a held-out set the build team did not tune against
  2. Known failure modesmaterial failures are understood and have defined handling
  3. Has a human fallbackand someone rostered to operate it
  4. Is observable in productiontraces retained, quality measurable after go-live
  5. Has a re-evaluation triggermaterial change or performance drift triggers re-testing

Why it matters

Every one of these is cheap to add during build and expensive to retrofit after go-live.

Four of the five are things assurance will ask you for anyway. Writing them into done means you build them once, on your schedule, instead of twice on someone else’s.

Do this

  1. Define representative cases before build starts.
  2. Agree the performance threshold that matters to the business.
  3. Define important failure cases and what happens when they occur.
  4. Make the evaluation set a project artefact with a named owner.
  5. Use the agreed results as part of the decision to pilot or go live.

What good looks like

The business can explain what acceptable performance means, and the delivery team can demonstrate whether the AI meets it.

1. Decide whether an agent fits

An agent plans its own steps and acts through tools. That is useful when the path varies, and a liability when it does not.

Reaches for an agent

  • The number and order of steps changes case by case.
  • The work needs several systems or documents pulled together.
  • A person currently makes judgement calls mid-task.
  • Volume is high enough that the oversight cost is worth carrying.

Use something simpler

  • The steps are the same every time. Use a workflow.
  • One prompt and one response would do it. Use a model directly.
  • The task is a lookup. Use search or a database.
  • The only reason is that agents are available.

Write it down before you go further

  • The task, in one sentence, and the outcome that counts as success.
  • Why a workflow or single model call is not sufficient.
  • The named owner accountable for what the agent does.
  • What the agent must never do, in plain words.

2. Set guardrails before you build

Guardrails are cheaper to design than to retrofit. Settle all five before any code is written.

Guardrails and who signs them off
GuardrailWhat good looks likeTypically signed off by
PurposeA one-paragraph scope, plus an explicit out-of-scope list.Business owner
AuthorityActions the agent takes alone are listed. Everything else needs a person.Business owner and risk
DataNamed sources it may read. Nothing written or retained beyond the stated purpose.Privacy and data custodian
OversightEvery action logged with enough detail to reconstruct a decision.Governance and assurance
FallbackIt stops and hands to a named person rather than guessing.Business owner

Test for authority limits. If you cannot answer "what is the worst thing this agent could do if it misreads the situation, and who would notice", the limits are not set yet.

3. Run a safe pilot

A pilot is for collecting evidence, not for proving the idea was good.

Set the pilot up

  • A bounded population: one team, one queue, or one document type.
  • A fixed end date and a decision point in the calendar.
  • Baseline numbers from before the agent, so a comparison is possible.
  • A person reviewing output while the pilot runs, not afterwards.
  • A documented way to switch it off inside a day.

Collect this evidence

EvidenceWhy it decides the next step
How often a human corrected the agent, and what they correctedPatterns in corrections show whether the scope or the guardrails are wrong.
Cases the agent refused or escalatedToo few escalations usually means the fallback is not working.
Time from start to finished outcome, against baselineThe benefit claim gets tested here or not at all.
Cost per completed taskAgent costs scale with steps taken, not users. Volume changes the picture.
Anything a person found uncomfortable explaining to a customerThe earliest signal of a transparency or fairness problem.

4. Decide whether to scale

Scaling is a separate decision with its own evidence. A successful pilot is not automatically a mandate.

Gates to clear first

  • Correction rate is low enough that review stays affordable at full volume.
  • The AIAF assessment reflects the system as it actually behaved, not as designed.
  • Logging survives the volume increase and is retained per your records policy.
  • Someone owns the agent operationally, with time allocated, not goodwill.
  • Affected staff know what changed and what they are still accountable for.
  • The off switch has been tested by someone other than the person who built it.

If the risk rating changed during the pilot, reassess before scaling. Agents that gained tools, data sources or autonomy along the way are not the system that was originally assessed.

5. Operate and monitor

Agents drift. Models are updated, source systems change shape, and the work itself moves.

WatchReviewAct when
Correction and escalation ratesWeekly at first, then monthlyA sustained rise, or a fall to near zero
Cost per taskMonthlyUnit cost climbs without volume explaining it
Model or tool version changesOn every changeAny change ships without a re-test
Complaints and customer feedbackContinuouslyA theme appears, however small
Scope creepQuarterlyThe agent is doing work nobody approved

Production visibility

For an agent, logs should provide enough evidence to reconstruct important steps, including relevant model or tool calls, actions taken and human decisions.

Pause it when

  • It takes an action outside its stated authority, even once.
  • You cannot reconstruct how it reached a decision from the logs.
  • The accountable owner leaves and nobody has picked it up.

6. Risks specific to agents

These are on top of the usual AI risks, not instead of them.

RiskWhat it looks likePractical control
Compounding errorA wrong step early becomes a confident wrong outcome later.Checkpoint the plan before actions are taken, not just the result.
Over-reachThe agent finds a tool that technically works and uses it.Allow-list tools. Deny by default.
Prompt injection through contentInstructions hidden in a document or email get followed.Treat all retrieved content as untrusted. Never let content grant authority.
Silent degradationQuality falls slowly and nobody is watching the trend.Sample and review output on a schedule, not on complaint.
Accountability gapNobody can say who decided, the agent or the officer.Record the human decision point explicitly in the log.
Automation biasReviewers approve because the agent proposed it.Measure how often reviewers disagree. Near zero is a warning.

7. Before you go live

One page to take to your governance forum.

  • Purpose and out-of-scope list written down and agreed.
  • Named accountable owner, with the role recorded, not just the person.
  • Authority limits explicit, with every autonomous action listed.
  • Data sources allow-listed. Retention agreed with the data custodian.
  • Logging captures inputs, plan, actions, outputs and human decisions.
  • Fallback path tested, including who receives the handover.
  • AIAF assessment complete and current.
  • AIRC referral made if the use case is high or critical risk.
  • Monitoring owner and review cadence set.
  • Off switch tested end to end.
  • Affected staff briefed on what they remain accountable for.

Save or print

Know what each stage proves

A working PoC does not prove that a solution is ready for production. Each stage should answer a different question.

  1. PrototypeExplore what the solution could look like.
  2. PoCProve AI can perform the intended task under controlled conditions.
  3. PilotTest with real users, data, controls and operating conditions.
  4. ProductionOperate reliably, sustainably and economically at scale.

Strong PoC results are evidence of technical feasibility, not production readiness. Production also requires reliable performance, clear ownership, controls, monitoring, sustainable cost and operational resilience.

Set an AI production definition of done

Agree these before the build is treated as production ready.

  • Meets the agreed performance threshold using representative test cases the build was not tuned against.
  • Material failure modes are understood and have defined handling.
  • A human fallback exists and someone is responsible for operating it.
  • Production behaviour can be observed and quality can be measured after go-live.
  • Material change or performance drift triggers re-testing.

What good looks like

The business defines what acceptable performance looks like. The delivery team can demonstrate that the system meets it before release.

Put the whole AI system under change control

AI behaviour can change even when the application code does not. Track the parts that can materially change behaviour.

What to keep under change control
PartWhat can changeWhat to do
ModelVersion, provider or routingRecord the version in use and assess material model changes.
PromptSystem instructions and promptsVersion important prompts and review material changes before release.
ContextRetrieved data, knowledge and source contentKnow which sources are used and re-test when material sources change.
ToolsSystems, APIs and actions available to the AIControl what the AI can access or do and reassess new capabilities.
PolicyGuardrails, thresholds and decision rulesTreat governance changes as system changes where they affect behaviour.

Where a vendor can change the underlying model or AI service, know:

  • what notice you receive
  • what change triggers re-testing
  • what you can roll back

Design for operation before go-live

Do not wait until production to work out how you will understand performance, incidents or cost.

Minimum production visibility

  • You can trace a request through the important processing steps.
  • Model calls, tool calls and retrieval steps can be identified where needed for troubleshooting and assurance.
  • Relevant inputs, outputs and actions can be reconstructed within privacy, security and records requirements.
  • Quality signals are measured after go-live, not only during testing.
  • Cost can be measured at a useful unit, such as cost per completed task.

If you cannot explain what happened when something goes wrong, it will be difficult to manage quality, assurance, incidents or cost.

Plan for cost to change with adoption

Traditional digital services often have a large build cost and relatively stable operating cost. AI consumption can increase with every user, document, model call or agent step.

  • Forecast operating cost as a range, not a single fixed number.
  • Track unit cost as adoption grows.
  • Reforecast when usage, models or solution design materially change.

Success can increase AI operating cost. Benefits and consumption therefore need to be monitored together.
